# The Geneva forecast for 1533

**Anthoine Brelochs au Lecteur salut**, the entire reader’s address on [A1]v. The title identifies a translation from German into French, names the physician of Hall, and gives the forecast year as 1533. The Geneva catalogue assigns printing to Gabriel Pomar in [1532]. The name is spelled *Breelochs* on the title and *Brelochs* in the address. Lökkös describes the reconstruction of the booklet from three incomplete sheets of printer’s waste found in a missal binding. This material history helps explain the damaged readings. The colophon credits translation and printing to Gabriel Pomar. The German copy for this year awaits comparison. ## English translation

**1**  Antoine Brelochs sends greetings to the reader.

**2–8**  I cannot marvel enough at the minds and thoughts of the people of our day, fallen into such great ignorance. They would sooner dwell on things down here and set their hearts and minds on them than on heavenly things. Surely we see God’s anger against us, the heavens in motion, and people’s minds going out of their senses. They rise against us like reckless people in a rage.

**8–13**  Even so, we are unwilling to correct and amend the understanding and counsel of our predecessors. We would sooner give free rein to the inclinations of the heavens, which have force and vigor in us, asking none of God’s grace or help until we see ourselves and our possessions brought to nothing by our enemies.

**13–15**  But listen, I pray you. This Ptolemy [possibly “this pagan Ptolemy”], that is, the prince of astrologers, says in his Almagest [reading uncertain] that the wise man will be lord and master of the stars and planets.

**15–20**  Surely a prudent physician skilled in the signs of the heavens recognizes, from signs of anger, hot and dry blood boiling in the human body. Will he not immediately change the quality of such a body to coldness and moisture, so that this force can no longer exert itself and there can instead be a proper balance, in which all our health consists?

**21–23**  In the same way the fury of adversaries can readily be appeased through circumspection and wisdom. Anyone, however slow of understanding, can readily recognize this from what has been said.

**24–31**  These things being so, moved more by pity for the commonwealth than by vainglory, I have wished to draw out some obscure matters from the teachings and rules of learned astrologers. These show us clearly how, in this year 1533, God’s fragile vessels will be led by the various motions of the heavens. Once these evils are foreseen, we can readily avoid them through human prudence, to the praise of God. Such praise I desire from the depths of my heart for all true Christians.

**32–34**  I ask you to receive this briefly worded prognostication in good part, taking and interpreting it in the spirit in which I wrote it. I bid you farewell.

## Notes on the reading

- **6–9.** The neighbors of the obscure word after *celestes* permit several punctuations. “The heavens in motion” is provisional. The shift from people who rise “against us” to a self-accusing “we” is preserved. “Of our predecessors” translates the awkward printed construction.
- **13–15.** Magnification shows an abbreviated word consistent with Lökkös’s *esnicque*. The historical French *etnique/ethnicque* could mean “pagan,” giving the possible sense “this pagan Ptolemy.” This lexical parallel leaves the spelling and identification provisional. [Académie française, *ethnique*, historical senses](https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A9E2884). Lökkös reads a reference to the *Almagest*. The attribution belongs to the French speaker. The maxim itself is clearly legible. An identification of its ultimate textual source would require separate evidence.
- **15–23.** *Qualité*, coldness, moisture and *moderation* belong to the contemporary account of humoral balance. *Cautelle* is rendered “circumspection” in the political analogy. The *aduersayres* are people whose anger can be appeased. The text supplies no reason to identify them as critics of astrology.
- **24–34.** The purpose clause presents pity for the commonwealth and praise of God as motives. The close is damaged after *dis-* and supplied with the aid of Lökkös. The next rubric concerns a lunar eclipse.
