# What changes in Brelochs’s implied polemic?

Method and completed comparative study • November 20, 2026 • Reassessment with the complete working translations

**Result.** The retrieved prefaces show a changing selection and elaboration of problems that astrology must answer. They offer weaker evidence that the criticisms actually voiced by contemporaries changed. The most defensible historical argument is about **changing apologetic emphasis within a continuing repertoire**, with the chronology of external criticism still to be established.

This study tests the proposal that criticism can be reconstructed through implied polemic. It does not assume that an author's imagined opponent is identical to a historical critic. The findings support the method's usefulness while identifying which historical conclusions this particular corpus can sustain.

## 1. Corpus and the question being tested

The corpus comprises eleven distinct forecast years: **1528, 1529, 1531, 1532, 1535, 1538, 1542, 1547, 1557, 1558 and 1559**. Twelve German TEI files represent these texts, because 1535 has a second, text-identical transcribed witness. That copy is useful for collation but is counted once for chronological inference. The [English translations](../translations/README.md) supply a reading aid; the German remains the evidential basis.

The years in the filenames are forecast years, generally later than the dedication dates. For example, the 1529 preface is dated June 24, 1528; the 1547 preface May 15, 1546; the 1557 preface June 8, 1556; and the 1558 preface June 24, 1557. The uncertain 1528 date and anomalous printed 1535 date remain unresolved. Claims about historical reactions must use this distinction: the preface for 1547 cannot casually be made a response to events that happened in 1547.

Three propositions need separate tests:

1. **Textual variation:** Brelochs presents different defenses in different dedications.
2. **Changed problematization:** the objections he chooses to anticipate, and their relative prominence, change.
3. **Changed external criticism:** those differences reflect a chronological change in objections actually being made against astrology or against Brelochs.

The first two are accessible through close comparison. The third needs corroboration, especially when a defense does not report an accusation. The corpus is an availability sample of retrieved editions; it is not an uninterrupted annual series.

## 2. Method planned and applied

The procedure combines **qualitative content analysis**, **reconstruction of arguments**, and **comparison of recurrent wording and dedicatory conventions**. Mayring's account of qualitative content analysis supplies the methodological basis for explicit units, category definitions, revision of coding rules, and an inspectable chain from text to inference. The procedure here is an adaptation to historical prefaces, not a claim to have fulfilled an independently validated measurement protocol.[1]

The unit of analysis is an argumentative passage, normally a claim together with its reason or qualification. The whole dedication is the context unit, so a sentence is not assigned an interpretation that its continuation contradicts. Twenty-five passage records from the initial study remain available in [coded-passages.csv](coded-passages.csv). This reassessment adds [implied-objections.csv](implied-objections.csv), ten records stating the candidate objection or audience response, its textual basis, Brelochs's defense, and the limit of the inference.

Two questions are coded separately: **What does Brelochs assert?** and **What evidence is there that someone opposed it?** This prevents an explicit defense from automatically becoming an explicit historical accusation.

| Class | Coding rule | Anchor example | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| E: reported criticism | Brelochs says people disparage the art or its publications | 1529: practicas regarded as “lügen büchlein” | A report by Brelochs that criticism existed; its grounds and representativeness require examination |
| R: staged objection | Brelochs formulates a conditional concession or objection without reporting who made it | 1529: criticism would be justified if astrology constrained God | An objection is explicitly available in his argument |
| I: inferred objection | A positive defense suggests a possible contrary proposition | 1558: stronger causes can prevent stellar effects | A plausible problem answered, also compatible with exposition or a commonplace |
| E-behavior: reported response | The audience is said to ignore or resist warnings | 1547 and 1557: growing stubbornness | A representation of conduct, distinct from a critic's proposition |
| N: local absence | The dedication offers presentation and reception formulas without a developed defense | 1531, 1532, 1535, 1538, 1559 | Local silence within this paratext |

The new register also records **authorial form** (such as explicit disclaimer or positive warrant) and **critic attribution** separately. An explicit qualification can therefore be recorded without attributing its contrary proposition to an actual opponent. Topic and evidence class are not interchangeable. For instance, **the 1542 statement of human noncompulsion is explicit; an accusation of determinism is inferred**. Likewise, a request that patrons interpret the work favorably is insufficient by itself to code an accusation. Technical terms, professional credentials in signatures, and general declarations of public benefit are not treated as developed rebuttals without an accompanying argument.

The comparison then tests four alternatives: repetition of an established repertoire; different space and function in short presentation letters; professional and patronal positioning; and gaps or variation between printings. No absent year is interpolated. No category frequency is treated as a measure of how common an objection was in society.

The study has now been carried out in two passes: the original passage coding and a reassessment against the complete translations and German texts. Two additional AI readings were instructed to use the primary texts without consulting the existing report: one examined the attribution of criticism, the other repetition and alternative explanations. Their convergence is documented in [review-log.md](review-log.md). This is an additional check on interpretation, **not independent human corroboration, formal double coding, or a statistical reliability estimate**. The German transcriptions still require scholarly collation.

## 3. Results across the series

| Forecast year(s) | What the dedication does | Candidate problem answered | Evidence concerning opponents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1528 | Authorizes knowledge through creation, learned tradition and practical prevention | Can celestial knowledge be legitimate and useful? | Inferred; no critic identified |
| 1529 | Reports disrepute, stages a theological objection, distinguishes God from his created order | Are practicas false? Does astrology constrain God or displace Christian trust? | Reported disrepute; staged theological objection; other objections inferred |
| 1531, 1532, 1535, 1538 | Presents the annual work to civic patrons | No developed problematization in the dedication | Local absence |
| 1542 | Reports contempt; defends noncompulsive influence through medical and natural examples | Is the art respectable and necessary for physicians? Does stellar government compel people? | Reported contempt; specific objections inferred |
| 1547 | Appeals to remembered calamities and rebukes neglected warnings | Why do people fail to respond? | Represented audience behavior |
| 1557 | Repeats and abbreviates the 1547 sequence | The same reception problem | Represented audience behavior; marked textual relationship |
| 1558 | Contrasts regular celestial motions with obstructible earthly effects; argues for prevention | How can real influences coexist with effects that fail to occur or are averted? | Inferred; no particular failed prediction reported |
| 1559 | Returns to customary presentation | No developed problematization in the dedication | Local absence |

### The early evidence already limits necessity

The 1528 dedication is a substantial positive authorization of the art. Creation makes the heavens useful and readable; philosophical, biblical and Christian authorities legitimate that reading. Its closing argument also says a person can prevent evil through astrological knowledge (A2v, lines 4–7). The forecast is offered “Nit das es von nöten sey allwegen / also zů beschehn/sonder zu eyner warnung” (A2v, lines 9–10): not because events must always happen so, but as a warning.

This is a decisive counterexample to a simple progression from early determinism to late contingency. A small uncertain clause at A2v, lines 2–3 complicates one description of avoiding misfortune, but the clear prevention statement in lines 5–6 supports the finding independently. The conclusion does not rest on repairing that uncertain clause.

### 1529 distinguishes the reported scandal from its proposed explanation

The dedication for 1529 reports that German practicas have acquired a bad reputation and have been regarded as “lügen büchlein” (A1v, lines 6–8). This is the strongest evidence for a represented public criticism in the collection. It does not identify particular failed forecasts or explain what “lies” means to the accusers.

The next move is conditional: if astrology were to encroach upon God's power and providence, such disrepute would be understandable (A1v, lines 8–22). Brelochs answers by separating knowledge of God's ordinary created operation from any limitation upon God. He also places action under Christ and tells the pious to fear God rather than the stars (A1v, lines 23–48; A2r, lines 1–10).

The implied polemic is thus richer than the reported accusation. Brelochs turns a reputational problem into a question of what legitimate astrology claims. This supports a close study of his defensive construction of the controversy. It does not establish that all those who called practicas false did so because they believed astrology restricted God. Nor does it identify Luther, Brenz, or any other particular contemporary as the target.

### 1542 relocates the defense within medical expertise

In 1542 Brelochs reports contempt by “etliche neydige geschwetzige menschen” (A1v, lines 9–11). The characterization is his; the historian should not adopt envy as the established cause of criticism.

He explicitly denies coercion: the stars “alleyn naygt vnd nit nöttigt” (A1v, lines 12–14). He then argues through birth and character, illness, plants, minerals and remedies, ending with the claim that every physician should know the art (lines 15–36). The prominence of professional medicine is a real change relative to the retrieved early dedications. The evidence establishes a change in the resources used to legitimate the art, while leaving the profession and exact reasoning of its critics unknown.

Importantly, natural examples are not new in 1542. The 1529 dedication already reasons from seasonal growth to wider celestial influence, even including peace and war among the proposed natural effects (A1v, lines 31–39). The later medical argument concentrates and develops an existing explanatory approach.

### 1547 and 1557 shift attention toward reception

The 1547 sequence is: Ptolemaic authority; celestial causes of remembered calamities; earlier warnings; failure to heed them; worsening obstinacy; comparison with the prophets; renewed prognostication (A1v, lines 6–28). Church admonition about sinful life is expressly included (lines 18–25). The author uses “we/us,” placing himself within the rebuked community rather than simply describing an external camp of hostile critics.

The 1557 preface reproduces the same distinctive sequence (A2r, lines 10–28). It abbreviates the church-rebuke expansion, retains the prophetic comparison, and adds the welfare of the German nation to the statement of benefit. These differences are observable; reasons such as political change, confessional change or secularization are not established by the omission or addition alone.

The strongest finding here concerns **uptake of warnings**, not a new proposition against astrology. Repetition prevents treating the two prefaces as independent evidence that a new objection arose twice. Equally, repetition does not show that the old argument had ceased to be relevant: a reused passage could answer renewed pressure. Establishing that renewed pressure requires external evidence.

### 1558 elaborates a mechanism of contingency

In 1558 the opening questions ask who can reverse the heavenly motions or prevent eclipses; the answer excepts God alone (A2r, lines 10–28, continuing A2v, line 1). The continuation then explains that stronger opposing causes can prevent indicated terrestrial outcomes (A2v, lines 12–20). The magnet-and-garlic and wound-and-treatment analogies make that obstruction intelligible within the author's natural philosophy. Navigation, farming and husbandry support the usefulness of observation and prevention (lines 21–29).

This would answer a question about unrealized predictions. But the preface reports neither a specific predictive failure nor an accuser advancing that objection. A causal explanation of conditional outcomes can also be an exposition of the art's ordinary principles.

The difficult phrase concerning a contrary cause “der öbersten Cörper” should not be forced into a definite physical theory. The clearer subsequent sentence, stating that a stronger cause may oppose the stars, suffices for the broad contrast. A comparison with 1542 makes the change more precise: there magnetic attraction exemplifies a hidden natural efficacy attributed to celestial powers (A1v, lines 25–33); in 1558 the magnet allegedly weakened by garlic illustrates the obstruction of an otherwise effective cause. The example is retained but given a different argumentative task. This is stronger evidence of changed defensive function than a simple count of natural-philosophical examples.

The explanation also remains expressly theological: the sun, moon and stars are God's creation and the Redeemer's footprints. The passage does not support a change from theological to secular justification.

## 4. A finer comparison: three limits on necessity

A general code such as “anti-determinism” would obscure distinctions important for the proposed study.

| Limit | Principal passage | What is protected | What it does not by itself establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| **God is not confined by the created order** | 1529 A1v40–48; A2r1–3 | Divine power and providence | The position of an identifiable theological opponent |
| **Stellar influence inclines without compelling** | 1542 A1v11–17 | Human beings from a coercive account of celestial government | A complete account of human freedom or a specific confessional doctrine |
| **Indicated effects may be obstructed or averted** | 1528 A2v4–10; 1558 A2v12–29 | Practical prevention and causal conditionality | A new concession made only after a documented failed forecast |

The modes of qualification are not identical, and their changing elaboration deserves analysis. That is a positive result for studying implied polemic. Their presence across the series also resists a sequence in which each new defense entirely replaces its predecessor.

## 5. Tests against alternative explanations

| Test | Observation | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Admit only explicit reports of disparagement | 1529 and 1542 remain; the latter supplies no specific proposition | Insufficient for a history of changing critics' arguments |
| Add author-staged and inferred objections | Distinct theological, professional and causal emphases become visible | Supports changing problematization, with lower confidence about external criticism |
| Compare only developed dedications | Differences remain among 1528, 1529, 1542, 1547/1557 and 1558 | Shortness alone does not explain the changes |
| Include short texts | Five dedicatory letters lack developed defenses; 1559 is a late example | The series does not steadily intensify in polemic |
| Check recurrence before claiming novelty | Prevention is already explicit in 1528; 1547/1557 share a sequence | Supports an enduring repertoire with varied deployment |
| Inspect stable patronal setting | Dedications repeatedly address Schwäbisch Hall's civic leadership | Limits one source of variation, without proving an unchanged public or production process |
| Inspect the dates of composition | Dedications usually precede the forecast year | Blocks premature association with events in the forecast year |
| Keep missing texts outside the matrix | 1548 remains a secondary-source lead | Prevents an unretrieved passage from becoming a new observed phase |

These are comparative checks, not experimental controls capable of isolating causation. Similarly, word counts indicate available textual space, not the intensity of controversy. Conventional requests for favorable reception occur in brief texts and cannot be treated as hidden attacks without further grounds.

## 6. What would test the external historical hypothesis more strongly?

Green identifies a response to a necromancy charge in **Brelochs's 1548 practica, A1v, VD16 ZV 2416**.[2] The recovered title-page photograph verifies the object and copy, but does not supply the preface. It therefore remains excluded from the observed chronology; see the [1548 source follow-up](1548-FOLLOWUP.md).

The highest-value next comparison is the wording of A1v and the first chapter of that edition. Does Brelochs report that someone accused him, stage a hypothetical objection, or merely assert the natural foundations of the art? Record the historical term before equating it with a modern definition of necromancy. If an independently identifiable accusation is present, compare its date and vocabulary with the defense. If the argument is conventional or reused, a claim of chronological novelty becomes weaker.

Other priorities follow from the observed gaps: 1530 for persistence of the 1529 defense; 1543–1546 for the interval between the medical and admonitory arguments; and 1553/1556 for the ancestry of the 1557 reprise. Other printings of the same year, especially 1542, should be collated before assigning all variation to the author. Whole-book reading is also required: a short dedication does not imply an absence of apology later in the practica.

External evidence would be especially probative if it connects a dated criticism, an identifiable target and a corresponding new response. General anti-astrological literature can establish a repertoire of contemporary objections, but cannot by itself identify Brelochs's opponent. No reproduction request or message has been sent as part of this analysis.

## 7. Conclusion suitable for further research

The available corpus supports the following working thesis:

> Between the prefaces for 1528 and 1559, Brelochs varied the problems through which he defended astrological prognostication: its Christian permissibility, its standing as natural and medical knowledge, its conditional usefulness, and its audience's obligation to heed warnings. These emphases recur and overlap. The prefaces therefore establish a history of defensive selection and elaboration more securely than a chronology of changing objections voiced by identifiable critics.

The proposed hypothesis is **partly supported at the level of implied polemic and unresolved at the level of external criticism**. It has not been disproved by the surviving evidence. Its strongest extension would identify occasions when an older defense was reactivated, as well as occasions when a new objection or new formulation can actually be documented.
