# What the expanded census changes about the polemic

20 November 2026 · [Census and source trail](../census/README.md) · [French text, translation and facsimiles](../comparanda/1533-geneva/README.md) · [Earlier German study](HYPOTHESIS-TEST.md)

**The 1533 Geneva address weakens a simple developmental reading.** Five years after the 1528 forecast says that foreknowledge can avert harm, a French booklet issued under Brelochs’s name illustrates that proposition with a physician treating an angry patient. The speaker passes from treatment to the management of enemies, having already rebuked a Christian “we” for yielding to celestial inclinations without seeking divine help. What distinguished the 1542 and 1547 prefaces in the original sample is already present in combination in this earlier print. Each later preface still makes a distinct argument. The evidence for a chronological change in what *critics themselves said* continues to rest on two unspecific authorial reports, for 1529 and 1542.

## Comparison and controls

The year is the year forecast. The unit of interpretation is one argument in its complete opening address. The census contains secure and historical title attestations for 26 years, but only twelve years contribute directly readable openings. Fourteen attested years therefore supply no text to this comparison. The missing opening of a located edition has no polemic code. In particular, the 1548 necromancy claim remains a secondary pointer to A1v, and the 1549 Lyon catalogue describes a title, audience and format. Neither can supply an opponent’s words. ## Three comparisons that bear on the hypothesis

**1528, 1533 and 1558 address an apparent paradox.** The WLS project’s Augsburg source for 1528 invokes Ptolemy’s book of a hundred sayings and explains that celestial knowledge enables people to prevent harm (A2v 3–10). In 1533 the reader meets the proposition as a question about treatment. A physician recognizes the hot, dry blood of anger and alters the body toward coldness and moisture ([A1]v 15–20). The next sentence extends the analogy to another person’s anger, which caution and wisdom can appease (21–23). The conclusion generalizes the example into a reason to publish forecasts (27–31). In 1558 treatment of a wound illustrates the operation of a stronger countercause alongside the magnet and garlic. Ptolemy and the wise man return near the conclusion (A2v 12–29). Prevention is visible throughout. The physician in 1533 offers a concrete bridge between an early claim about warning and a later account of competing causes. The similar moves justify comparison of arguments across languages. Verbal borrowing would require a German counterpart or closer evidence of textual dependence.

**1533 complicates the move from theology to medicine.** In the WLS 1529 dedication the speaker answers a reported loss of credibility by distinguishing God from the ordinary operation of creation. The 1533 opening places prayer, real celestial inclinations and a physician’s intervention in one sequence. Human prudence works within that order, and the avoidance of foreseen evils ends in praise of God. In the 1542 Augsburg dedication, by contrast, the claim that a physician should know astrology lends the art professional authority (A1v 11–36). The physician’s role changes across these two passages. In 1533 a competent practitioner demonstrates the possibility of intervention. In 1542 medicine helps defend the standing of the discipline. Treating both simply as “medical polemic” would conceal the difference.

**1533 also complicates the move from defending astrology to blaming its audience.** Its speaker accuses “us” of letting celestial inclinations work unchecked and deferring an appeal for divine aid until enemies have done their worst ([A1]v 8–13). The 1547 dedication looks back over remembered disasters and earlier warnings, compares stubborn listeners with those who resisted the prophets, and presents a renewed forecast (A1v 15–28). The 1557 dedication reuses the sequence (A2r 19–28). Only the latter pair explicitly supplies the retrospect and prophetic comparison. None of these three passages quotes an opponent of astrology. A Christian rebuke can be central to an astrologer’s preface even when no critic appears.

## What the record will bear

The strongest formulation is that **different openings assign different argumentative jobs to a continuing set of defenses**. Prayer and human judgment, professional competence, and the possibility of averting harm can coexist. Short civic presentation letters for 1531, 1532, 1535 and 1538 once made the early 1530s look quiet. A developed reader’s address now falls in their midst. Two variables change together, language and addressee. The title advertises translation from German, but the identity of the underlying text and the extent of editorial adaptation have yet to be established. Accordingly, the medical example can be dated in **French print for the forecast year 1533**, not securely assigned verbatim to Brelochs’s German dedication.

The most discriminating next comparison is already tied to an object. Germanisches Nationalmuseum **Postinc 8º Nw 2871** contains German forecasts for 1533, 1534, 1540 and 1546 according to Green. If the German 1533 likewise joins prayer, the physician and appeasing enemies, the argument can be located in both transmissions. If it contains a civic gift letter, juxtaposition of the editions will show what was changed for a French readership. If both opening texts occur in one copy, the unit of analysis must include position in the booklet as well as language and audience. The same volume offers neighboring years to test persistence. The 1548 A1v and the 1556 preface provide independent tests of alleged necromancy and the approach to the 1557 reuse. None of these alternatives is counted as an observed result. The comparison uses a working French transcription with four places marked uncertain and the damaged farewell supplied. The main conclusions depend on the clear clause about divine help, the physician’s example, the appeasement of adversaries and the utility claim. They survive uncertainty about the epithet or title of Ptolemy’s work. The available material cannot establish the prevalence of a criticism in society or a year in which it first arose.
