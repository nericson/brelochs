<!-- English segment 2; source range line-1557-1-7 through line-1557-2-2, inclusive. -->
Steadfast, honorable, prudent, and wise lords: first I offer Your Worships my entirely willing and diligent service. Gracious and beloved lords in authority,

Ptolemy, a highly renowned teacher in the art of the stars, explains and describes in his little book of a hundred sayings how the changes of the bodies of this lower world, now into one state and then into another, take place through the revolutions and changes of the heavenly bodies. We see this daily and have now experienced it for many years: the courses of the seven wandering stars and of other stars have caused many great uprisings, violent wars, severe and deadly epidemics, and terrible and prolonged dearth. Although many people sufficiently indicated and reported these things as warnings some years ago, no one has taken them to heart up to now; instead, people have continually become more obstinate, just as similar things happened frequently in the times of the prophets, etc.

Accordingly, once again, to please Your Worships humbly, for everyone's benefit and for the welfare of the German nation as a whole, I have undertaken to describe as briefly as possible in this prognostication of mine what good or evil the stars indicate and threaten for us in this year 1557. I ask Your Worships to receive this from me and interpret it as favorably as possible, and to keep me, your humble servant, in your gracious care.

## Translation notes

1. The civic honorific E.F.W. is rendered 'Your Worships', a collective form of address to the magistrates; Stetmaystern is rendered 'mayors'.

2. The seven 'wandering stars' are the seven planets of the historical cosmological scheme, including the sun and moon. This vocabulary is retained rather than converted into the modern astronomical classification.

3. The phrase '56.der mindern zale' gives a shortened year. The supplied century in '[15]56' makes the date readable while marking what is not printed in full.

4. The little book of a hundred sayings is attributed to Ptolemy by Brelochs; the attribution is translated without independent authentication. Thewrung is rendered 'dearth', retaining the sense of scarcity and high prices.
