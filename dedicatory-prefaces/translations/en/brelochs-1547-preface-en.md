<!-- English segment 2; source range line-1547-1-4 through line-1547-1-28, inclusive. -->
Steadfast, honorable, prudent, and wise lords: first I offer Your Worships my entirely willing and diligent service. Gracious and beloved lords in authority,

Claudius Ptolemy of Pelusium, a highly renowned teacher in the art of the stars, writes and explains in his little book of a hundred sayings that the changes of the bodies of this lower world, now into one state and then into another, take place through the revolutions and changes of the heavenly bodies and through their influence. We see this daily and have now experienced it for many years: the movement and courses of the seven planets and other heavenly stars have caused many great uprisings, wars, severe and deadly epidemics, as well as terrible and prolonged dearth, fire, envy, hatred, and other such evils.

Many people have sufficiently reported and indicated these things as warnings in past years. Yet no one has taken all this to heart up to now, nor does anyone do so even now. Rather, the more we are warned—and especially the more earnestly we are exhorted and rebuked in church because of our wicked, grievous, and sinful lives—the more slowly and less readily we set about amending our lives; instead, we are continually found to be more obstinate. Similar things happened frequently in the times of the prophets, and greater and more notable calamity and harm generally followed, as Holy Scripture sufficiently shows and gives us to understand in many places.

Accordingly, once again, to please Your Worships humbly, for your benefit and for everyone's welfare, I have described in this prognostication of mine what future good or evil the stars threaten and indicate for us in this year 1547.

## Translation notes

1. The civic honorific E.F.W. is rendered 'Your Worships'; it functions as a collective form of address to the magistrates, rather than a claim about their individual wisdom. Stetmeyster is rendered 'mayors'.

2. The 'little book of a hundred sayings' is the work Brelochs attributes to Ptolemy. The translation preserves that attribution without independently establishing its authorship.

3. Theurung denotes dearth or scarcity expressed in high prices. 'Dearth' preserves the economic sense without restricting the term to complete famine.

4. The source's final relative clause about the eight chapters is loosely attached after the request for protection. The translation makes the intended reference to the prognostication explicit by starting a new sentence.
