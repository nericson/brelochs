Steadfast, honorable, prudent, and wise lords: I first offer Your Worships my entirely willing and diligent service. Gracious and beloved lords in authority: Since through daily experience we learn and see that this lower world, including human beings, irrational animals, stones, plants, and the like, is governed by the stars and the course of the heavens, it is surely fitting that this noble art of astrology should be accepted among the other arts and in no way held in contempt—as so often happens at the hands of certain envious, garrulous people.

That we human beings are governed by the stars needs little or no proof when we carefully observe and consider the nature and character of one person and then another whose time of birth is known, finding them inclined according to the nature and tendency of this or that planet. I speak here only of the tendency and influence of the stars, and not of their coercion or compulsion, since we well know that the stars only incline and do not compel. We may also observe moonstruck, mentally deranged people, who rage more at one time than at another according to the movements of the moon and the other stars. Likewise, people's illnesses and ailments increase or diminish according to the tendency and influence of the stars. Teachers of medicine discover this daily, provided that they have experience of this art of the stars, as Hippocrates and Galen teach and require. The flowers of chicory, too, habitually and for the most part turn toward the sun.

If one also asks all physicians and masters of natural philosophy [what] the reason is that a magnet attracts iron, or that an emerald breaks in the presence of unchastity because it cannot endure it, or again what makes turpeth, rhubarb, and many other plants, roots, seeds, and the like purge and expel, and so forth, they do not say that this happens because these things are cold, hot, moist, or dry. Rather, they say that its cause lies in the celestial powers, which have infused into them a secret nature, hidden from us, through which they possess a particular efficacy—as Mesue testifies in the book on the Consolation of Medicine.

Since, then, all this makes it sufficiently clear that every physician ought to have experience and knowledge of the art of the stars, as Hippocrates, Galen, Rhazes, and Guarinus teach and require, I, although the least among them, have once again, following the old custom and in humble service to Your Worships, compiled as briefly as possible a prognostication from the aforesaid art. In it I indicate what good and evil the stars portend for us in the coming year 1542. I ask Your Worships to receive it and interpret it in the best light, and to keep me, your poor and humble servant, in your care. Given at Schwäbisch Hall on St. Ulrich's Day, in the year 1541.

## Translation notes

1. The honorific E.F.W. is rendered 'Your Worships'; the expanded opening addresses the recipients' prudent wisdom (ewer fürsichtig(en) weyßheyt). Stettmayster is rendered 'mayors'; the precise distribution of civic offices is not established by this translation.

2. Astronomia is translated 'astrology' where it denotes the knowledge of stellar influences and prognostication defended in this passage. Brelochs's historical terminology overlaps the fields now distinguished as astronomy and astrology. The 'wandering star' in line 17 is rendered 'planet.'

3. The German syntax of lines 11–17 inserts the noncompulsion qualification inside the claim needing little or no proof. The English moves that qualification into a following sentence, preserving its scope while making the passage readable.

4. At line 25 the working transcription marks vrs as unclear. The scan appears to show an abbreviated form resembling wz, plausibly was ('what'). The translation therefore supplies '[what]' explicitly as a tentative reading; the German TEI has not been altered.

5. The medical, botanical, and mineral examples are translated as the author's historical assertions. 'Moonstruck' renders Monsüchten, retaining its asserted lunar connection. Turbit is rendered 'turpeth,' the purgative drug, and Reubarbarum as 'rhubarb.'

6. 'Consolation of Medicine' is a literal working rendering of the title or description attributed to Mesue (lines 32–33), not a verified identification of an edition or treatise. The abbreviated d̃ is understood as the article belonging to von der tröstung. Guarinus is retained without an unverified identification.

7. St. Ulrich's Day conventionally falls on July 4. The translation retains the printed feast-day dating rather than inserting an independently verified publication date.
